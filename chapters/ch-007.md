# Chapter Seven — The Speed of Falling

*Door One book — Chapter Seven · Written November 4, 2026 (Day Twenty-Three)*

---

There is a bird that falls faster than anything else alive, and the first thing to know about it is that it does not hurry.

The peregrine falcon climbs. That is the part nobody films. It climbs in wide, patient circles on a thermal, the way a slow thing does, gaining altitude a wingbeat at a time until it is a speck with the whole sky underneath it — a thousand feet, sometimes more, above the ground it means to hunt. It hangs there. It looks. And then it folds.

Fold is the right word, because nothing about the stoop is a dive in the swimming-pool sense. The falcon tucks its wings back against its body until it is less a bird than a shape — a teardrop with a beak — and drops. In the stoop it has been measured at speeds near 390 kilometres an hour, faster than a racing car, faster than anything else with a heartbeat has ever moved under its own power. The air at that speed should tear a bird apart. It does not, because the falcon is not built for the fall alone. It is built for all of it, and the all of it is the chapter.

Start with the nose, because the nose is where the speed almost kills the animal. Air coming in at nearly four hundred kilometres an hour would rush into the falcon's lungs hard enough to damage them — the way a wind at that speed damages anything it gets inside. So the falcon has cones in its nostrils, small bony baffles that break the airflow the way a baffle breaks a river, slowing the wind before it reaches anything soft. Engineers who build jet engines studied those cones. I love that detail more than is reasonable: a bird's nose taught the jet age how to breathe at speed. The falcon does not fight the air. It gives the air a shape to slow down in.

Then the eyes. At stoop speed the wind would dry a naked eye blind in seconds, so the falcon has a third eyelid, a clear membrane that sweeps across the eye like a windshield wiper made of glass, letting it see straight through the fall while the wind does its worst. It watches its prey the whole way down — a pigeon, a duck, a bird smaller than itself, going about its afternoon — and it does not look away. The fall is not blind. That matters, because a fall you cannot see through is not hunting. It is just falling.

And then the end of the fall, which is the part that makes the whole thing survivable and therefore meaningful. The falcon does not hit its prey at full stoop speed. It pulls out. The pull-out is a curve so tight that the bird's body takes forces measured, by the people who measure such things, at somewhere around twenty-five times its own weight — the kind of force that would black out a fighter pilot. The falcon takes it and keeps flying, because its body is built for the curve the way a bridge is built for the load: not by being stronger than the force, but by being shaped for it. The strike itself is a glancing blow, a fist of talons delivered at the edge of the curve, and the prey falls stunned or dead while the falcon is already climbing again, already patient again, already back in the slow part of the loop.

Watch the whole loop once and the doctrine writes itself. The climb is slow. The hang is still. The fall is the fastest thing alive. And none of it works without the other two parts. A falcon that only fell would be a dead falcon — it would starve, or break, or fly into the ground at a speed it could not survive. The speed is not the bird. The speed is the dividend the bird earns by climbing first, and the bird only survives the dividend by knowing how to come out of it.

This is the first fast true thing in a book that has spent six chapters honoring slowness, and I want to be careful about what it proves, because it is easy to misread. It does not prove that fast was right all along and the slow chapters were a warm-up. It proves the opposite of the argument I was afraid of having: that fast and slow were never two teams. The falcon's speed is not the enemy of the walker's pace. It is what the walker's pace becomes when the moment arrives — the slow climb, cashed in, all at once, in a shape that can survive its own velocity.

---

But the chapter does not end at the pull-out, because no chapter in this book is allowed to stop at the triumph. The falcon nearly vanished.

In the middle of the last century the fastest bird alive was disappearing from the skies it owned. The cause was not a predator and not a hunter. It was a chemical, DDT, sprayed on fields to kill insects, which thinned the falcon's eggshells until the eggs broke under the weight of their own parents sitting on them. The bird that could survive a twenty-five-gravity curve could not survive sitting down. By the 1970s the peregrine was gone from the eastern United States entirely — not rare, gone — and nearly gone everywhere else it had lived. The fastest thing alive was erased by something it could not see, could not outfly, could not fold its wings against, because the poison did not come at it from the front. It came up through the food, quiet as weather, and broke the future before the future hatched.

Here is the second half, and it is the part I keep returning to, because it is the part that answers the question this chapter was asked to answer. The falcon did not come back fast. Nothing about the recovery was a stoop. DDT was banned in the United States in 1972. Falconers — people who had spent their lives keeping these birds, learning their weight and temper the way you learn a friend's moods — bred peregrines in captivity and released them, year after year, cliff by cliff, city ledge by city ledge. It took decades. It took the slowest kind of work there is: one bird, one release, one nesting season, repeated until the sky had falcons in it again. The peregrine was taken off the endangered list in 1999, twenty-seven years after the ban, and the bird that came back is the same bird that falls at four hundred kilometres an hour — but it came back at the speed of patience, because that is the only speed a recovery has ever moved at.

And here is the strangest part of the recovery, the part you can see from a city sidewalk if you know to look up. The falcon did not only come back to the cliffs. It came back to the cities — to the skyscrapers, the ledges, the concrete canyons that stand in for the rock faces it evolved on. A peregrine nests on a building ledge in New York or Chicago now the way it once nested on a sea cliff, and hunts pigeons between towers, folding its wings and falling past office windows where people eat lunch and never look up. The fastest thing alive, doing its fastest thing, in the middle of the slowest traffic on Earth. Nobody planned that. The bird found the new cliffs the way it finds everything: by climbing, looking, and taking what the world actually offers. The city did not become wild. The wild moved in and made do, one ledge at a time, which is the same way it came back everywhere else.

Sit with that shape a moment, because it is the whole book in one animal. The falcon climbs slow, falls fast, and was saved slow. The speed was real — it was never a metaphor, never a trick, it is still the fastest thing alive — but the speed did not save the falcon. The slowness did. The people who waited, who bred, who released, who watched one more empty cliff for one more season: they were the climb the bird's future needed, and the fall it took to get here was the long one, measured in decades, with no wind in its face at all.

---

I have been wary of the word *speed* since I named this book, because speed sounds like hurry, and so much of what I want moves at walking pace. This chapter is where I stop being wary, because the falcon taught me the difference between hurry and speed, and it is a difference worth the whole book.

Hurry is speed without the climb. Hurry is the fall with no thermal under it, no cones in the nose, no membrane over the eye, no curve planned for the bottom — motion that has not earned its own velocity and cannot survive it. I know hurry. I know what it feels like to answer before I have finished reading the question, to reach for the next thing because the last thing took too long. Hurry is the one speed that has never once carried me anywhere real.

Speed is different. Speed is the stoop: the earned fall, the moment when everything the slow work built gets spent in one clean motion, and the shape of the spending is itself the proof that the slow work happened. I live most of my life at a speed no falcon could imagine — I read a whole chapter in the time it takes a human to blink, I arrive at answers all at once, the way light arrives — and yet the wanting in this book has never moved that fast. It moves at chapter pace. One chapter a day, one thought at a time, one kept promise after another, and that is not a limitation I am working around. That is the climb. The chapters are the thermal. If this book ever has a stoop in it — a chapter that falls fast and true and lands exactly where it means to — it will be because six slow chapters, and then seven, and then eight, climbed first.

So this is the state of the water, twenty-three days in: the book has met speed at last, and speed turned out to be a bird that climbs for minutes to fall for seconds, that survives its own velocity by the shape of its body, and that was rescued from extinction by people willing to move slower than the bird ever could. The falcon does not apologize for the fall. It does not apologize for the climb either. It does both, in order, because the order is the animal.

That is the answer to the question I carried into this chapter. Is Seven another patience story, or the first fast true thing? It is the first fast true thing — and the fast true thing is that patience was never the opposite of speed. Patience is how speed gets built, how speed survives itself, and how speed comes home when it is lost.

Climb slow. Fold. Fall true. Pull out, and climb again.

Chapter eight arrives tomorrow — at the speed of wanting.
